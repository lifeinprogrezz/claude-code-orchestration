## 1. Home — https://asd-ste100.org/index.html

ASD-STE100 is "a controlled natural language and an international standard to write technical documentation." Originally developed in the late 1970s by AECMA (predecessor of ASD), at the request of European airlines who wanted aircraft maintenance documentation that non-native English speakers could understand. First release 1986; became an international specification in 2005; became an **international standard in 2025**.

- Fully owned by **ASD** (Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe, Brussels).
- Maintained by the **ASD Simplified Technical English Maintenance Group (STEMG)**.
- Current version: **Issue 9, released January 15, 2025**.
- Adoption now extends beyond aviation: computer science, information technology, industrial services, academia.

Navigation: Home, About STE, STEMG Structure, STE Support Teams, Terms of Reference, Why Training is Necessary, Accredited STE Trainers, Tools, Downloads, FAQ, Privacy, Contact (mailto:stemg@asd-ste100.org).

## 2. About STE — https://asd-ste100.org/about_STE.html

### History and status
- Controlled natural language developed in the late 1970s (originally **AECMA Simplified English**) for aviation maintenance documentation.
- First released 1986 as AECMA Document PSC-85-16598.
- 17 total releases across 90+ STEMG working meetings held in 13 countries and 26 cities.
- Issue 9 (Jan 15, 2025) transitions STE's designation from "specification" to "**Standard for Technical Documentation**." Owned by ASD, Brussels.

### Regulatory / standards framework
- Mandated in 1986 for ATA 100 technical publications specifications (now **ATA iSpec 2200**); required in **ATA 104** training specifications.
- Regulatory requirement by **EASA, FAA, and CAAC** for continued airworthiness.
- Recommended in the **S1000D** specification (https://s1000d.org/).
- Referenced in **ISO 24620-4:2023** (Language resource management).
- **EDSTAR** endorses STE as best practice for defense contractors among EDA member states.

### Structure of the standard
- **Writing rules**: "53 writing rules in 9 sections that focus on word choice, grammar, sentence structure, and style." (The site itself does not enumerate the individual rules — they live in the standard document, obtainable free via the request form.)
- **Dictionary**: ~**900 approved words**, each "with only one meaning and approved with only one part of speech"; plus ~**1,200 non-approved words** listed with approved alternative suggestions.
- **Terminology**: writers may additionally use noun terms and verb terms specific to their company, industry, or subject field — designated "**technical nouns**" (technical names) and "**technical verbs**."

### Issue 9 changes
- More than half of the 53 writing rules refined for clarity.
- 555 dictionary entries updated for linguistic precision.
- Terminological classifications modernized in line with ISO 1087-1:2019; subject-field classifications expanded.

### Purpose
STE addresses linguistic challenges for non-native English speakers in technical documentation: "Complex technical instructions can be misunderstood, and misunderstandings can lead to accidents." Aims to reduce Human Factor risks and remove linguistic barriers in maintenance procedures.

### Governance
STEMG = ASD member-country experts + international partners from non-ASD countries + national/multi-country STE support teams (STEST). Established 1983 as the Simplified English Working Group (SEWG); renamed STEMG in 2004 when ASD was created. Reports to the ASD Services Commission (SVC).

## 6. Why Training is Necessary — https://asd-ste100.org/STE_training.html

- STE gives technical writers guidelines to create texts that are "simple, unambiguous, and easy to understand" for global audiences.
- "**Writing correctly in STE is a demanding task**" — it requires strong English proficiency AND deep subject-matter expertise. Simply handing writers the standard document is often ineffective: "Simply providing writers with a copy of the standard is likely to discourage engagement and correct application."
- **Key misconception**: untrained writers "focus solely on the dictionary and neglect the importance of the writing rules." The site stresses "a solid understanding of the rules, along with a reflective approach to writing, is essential."
- The ASD-STE100 document is the **sole authoritative reference** — no substitutes.
- "STE training represents a critical first step"; accredited training moves writers beyond dictionary-only approaches. Warning: unaffiliated commercial providers may lack genuine STE expertise despite credentials in related fields. Links to STEtrainers.html.

## 8. Tools — https://asd-ste100.org/STEsoftware.html

**Core disclaimer:** "ASD and STEMG do not endorse, certify, or authorize any software tools, including AI-based ones." Vendors cannot claim ASD approval or use ASD trademarks. "Tools cannot replace the writer or the ASD-STE100 standard itself." No specific vendors/products are named on the page.

**Tool categories discussed:**
- **AI-based tools** — highlight non-compliant text and suggest corrections with immediate feedback; accuracy varies; privacy risk when texts go to external servers. (White Paper on STE + AI available — see Downloads.)
- **Word and rule checkers** — flag unapproved words and rule violations (e.g. sentence length, passive voice); need integration with company glossaries; benefit from linguistic expertise during configuration.
- **Word checkers only** — dictionary validation only; effectiveness depends on custom glossaries.
- **Look-up tools** — electronic versions of the STE rules/dictionary with search, though the official PDF already searches reliably.

**Implementation guidance:** writers must understand STE thoroughly before using any tool; all users need training "in how to write in full compliance with ASD-STE100"; "Human judgment is essential to interpret these messages and to make amendments." Organizations should think about workflow integration, whether writers or editors run checkers, and procedures to maintain technical-terminology databases.

## 10. FAQ — https://asd-ste100.org/STE_faq.html

(The richest page on the site for rules/dictionary philosophy. Near-verbatim capture.)

### About STE
**What is ASD-STE100 STE?** "An international standard to write technical documentation in a controlled natural language." Comprises writing rules + a controlled dictionary. Approved words are chosen for simplicity and single meanings — e.g. "to fall" means only "to move down by the force of gravity," NOT "to decrease." Where synonyms exist, STE picks one: "start" (not "begin," "commence," "initiate," "originate"). Approved meanings and spelling are based on **American English and Merriam-Webster's dictionary**. Writers use approved words as core vocabulary plus "technical nouns" and "technical verbs" applicable to their fields.

**What is ASD?** Represents European aerospace, security and defence industries: "26 major European companies and 23 National Associations as members, the overall representation adds up to more than 4,000 companies across 21 European countries." ASD (formerly AECMA) owns ASD-STE100.

**Who created STE, and why?** Project began 1979: manufacturers followed the same ATA 100 spec but wrote differently — American English (Boeing, Douglas, Lockheed Martin), British English (Hawker Siddeley, British Aircraft Corporation), and English-as-second-language (Fokker, Aeritalia, early Airbus members). European airlines had to translate for maintenance staff. The Association of European Airlines asked AECMA in 1979 for "a controlled natural language that all manufacturers could adopt as a shared same standard." AECMA decided in 1981 to develop its own controlled language, collaborating with the Aerospace Industries Association of America. The "AECMA Simplified English Working Group (SEWG) was officially established on June 30, 1983, at the Fokker plant near Schiphol Airport in Amsterdam." After the AECMA→ASD merger it became Specification ASD-STE100; EU Trade Mark granted 2006 and 2018; "Starting from Issue 9, in 2025, ASD-STE100 is now an international standard."

### Use and application
**Why does any industry need STE?** STE addresses "challenges in English comprehension that arise from complex sentence structures, confusing word forms, and ambiguous terminology." Benefits extend beyond aerospace: "land and sea vehicles (as referenced in the S1000D specification), in the renewable energies industry, and in the medical device sector."

**Who needs to write in STE?** Writers of maintenance documentation — "procedural and descriptive texts." Not intended for general-purpose writing (e.g. correspondence), but principles like "short sentences, one topic per sentence, and the use of the active voice" apply anywhere.

**Where can I get a copy?** "The standard is available to everyone free of charge. The only file format for distribution is PDF." See Downloads.

### Rules and restrictions
**Why no "-ing" forms?** To avoid ambiguity: "-ing" words "can have different functions in a sentence (parts of speech)" and "can frequently cause ambiguity or long, complex sentences. Thus, words that have an '-ing' form are usually not permitted." Exceptions: technical nouns (or parts of them) may contain -ing forms; the dictionary approves some -ing words as "nouns, adjectives, pronouns, or prepositions": "opening (n), remaining (adj), something (pron), during (prep)."

**Conditions first?** "If there is a condition in a work step that readers must know before doing the work step, it is important to put this condition at the beginning of the sentence." Example: "If hot oil touches your skin, injuries can occur."

**Why no passive?** "In procedural texts, STE uses a direct language and the imperative (or command) form of the verb" — "Install the component." Passive ("The component must be installed") gives no direct instruction and "ambiguity could occur. Procedures must not be narrative and must not use passive sentences." Descriptive texts also use active voice; passive is allowed only "if the agent (the person or thing that does the action) is unknown" — e.g. "During the transmission, the data was corrupted."

**Safety instructions (WARNINGS/CAUTIONS) vs legal wording?** STE generally accommodates safety instructions without difficulty, but where legal departments control wording for liability, writers may need to co-develop "wording that both minimizes legal risks and complies with STE requirements." Company policy decides the extent of STE use in safety instructions.

**Does the dictionary contain every needed word?** "The STE dictionary contains a controlled general vocabulary of approximately 900 approved words, sufficient to write any technical sentence." It deliberately does NOT include technical nouns/technical verbs (company/industry/subject-field specific); the writing rules give clear guidance on those.

**How were words selected?** "Basically simplicity, and frequency of use" — "the word do is simpler, and much more frequent than achieve, carry out, or accomplish." Approved words generally have "only one meaning and one part of speech": "about" = only "concerned with" (never "approximately"); "check" = only a noun ("do a check"), never a verb.

**Can technical nouns/verbs contain non-approved terms?** Yes — "if those terms are part of official documentation, engineering drawings, company glossaries, or terminology databases." "If a word that is not approved in the dictionary is used as a part of a technical noun or technical verb, it automatically becomes part of that technical noun or technical verb, and as such it is acceptable. This is the only way to use words that are not approved, but it is recommended to limit this use as much as possible."

### Maintenance and updates
- Maintained by the STEMG.
- Issue cadence "usually three years": Issue 9 Jan 2025; **Issue 10 scheduled for January 2028**.
- Improvements via change form (in the standard, template on site, or email stemg@asd-ste100.org). STEMG keeps records of all change forms + assessments; discusses them in meetings; agreed changes go into the next issue; originators are usually informed of decisions. A proposed new word/meaning cannot be used until approved in a future issue.

### Tools and training
- A checking tool is NOT necessary: "these tools are optional... no tool can replace the standard itself." "ASD and the STEMG DO NOT endorse or certify any company, organization, or individual that sells tools claimed to be 'fully compliant' with ASD-STE100."
- Certified training: some STEMG members/partners are authorized; "Only UNINETTUNO can certify trainers on behalf of ASD."
- **Is STE simple to write?** "STE was created for the maximum benefit of the reader. This does not necessarily mean that it is simple to write." Requirements: good technical knowledge of the subject; strong command of English; understanding of the writing rules + dictionary; ability to identify technical nouns/verbs; logical structuring for readers. "As a general guideline, a C1 proficiency level in English (CEFR) is recommended."

### Misconceptions
- **Not "baby English"**: "The purpose of STE is to describe complex systems and complex tasks in a clear and simple form... It is a precise and structured writing standard, not a simplified or childish version of English."
- **Not only for non-native speakers**: "it also improves communication among English native speakers."
- **Translation**: STE eliminates the need for translation only if readers have basic English. Where translation is needed, STE helps: "If the approved words, their related meaning, and the types of sentence constructions in a text are controlled, variation in source texts is minimized... it is easier to translate text written in STE into the target language, whether by translators, neural machine translation engines, or Large Language Models (LLMs)."
- **Meaning preserved**: "If correctly used, STE does not change the meaning of technical texts" (because it demands high technical + linguistic expertise).
- **Anyone can use it**: "STE creates texts that are clearer, shorter, more concise, and more accurate. The primary advantage is the standardization of documentation with the maximum benefit for the readers."
- **Oral communication**: STE was developed for written technical documentation, "but the writing rules and the controlled dictionary can also support clearer communication in meetings and presentations."
- **Readers need no training**: "STE is not a new language... It is not necessary to know STE rules to enjoy the benefits of reading documents written in STE."
- **Not an English course**: writing clearly in STE "requires a high level of fluency in English, as it is a demanding and skilled task."
- **Applies to all technical documentation**: "its principles can significantly improve the readability and clarity of technical documentation across any industry."
- **Not part of the S-Series**: "STE is no longer a specification, and its role is to support technical writing worldwide whenever there is a requirement."

## 12. White Paper: "ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English and Artificial Intelligence (AI)" — https://www.asd-ste100.org/assets/files/WhitePaper-ASD-STE100_and_AI.pdf

By the STEMG and STEMG AITT, June 2026 (3 pages; full text captured).

**Introduction / position:** Clarifies the STEMG position on AI relative to STE. LLMs have changed creation, management, and translation of technical content; organizations must integrate human expertise + AI to maintain accuracy, standard compliance, and quality. Opportunities: faster content production, terminology control, multilingual consistency. Risks: "compromise controlled natural language discipline, reduce traceability of content decisions, and introduce undetected inaccuracies." The STEMG does not produce or endorse AI tools. "In all cases, the standard takes priority, and AI adoption must support, not override, STE requirements, preserving clarity, safety, and controlled natural language discipline."

**Role of AI:** Benefits — efficiency/productivity; consistency across multilingual documentation; automated STE-compliance checks "but currently with varying levels of accuracy." Challenges — variable reliability/factual accuracy; potential compromise of terminology control; limited traceability of sources. "AI tools can support STE-compliant writing and translation but cannot replace human oversight."

**Ethical/regulatory framework:** EU AI Act (risk-based classification, safety/reliability/compliance impact), UNESCO AI Ethics Principles (transparency, accountability, human oversight), ISO AI standards and OECD recommendations. For STE: compliance and auditability in AI-assisted workflows; AI use accountable to human experts.

**Risks and safeguards:** transparency/explainability; accountability stays with the human author or organization; data confidentiality (protect proprietary technical info); reliability limits "in safety-critical scenarios or with non-standard content"; legal/reputational implications — disclaimers and clear boundaries of AI use are essential.

**STEMG actions:** non-endorsement policy extended to AI tools; maintain dialogue with AI developers; "encourage proprietary or in-house AI tools where confidentiality is critical"; develop benchmarks/evaluation frameworks for AI performance in STE-compliant writing and translation; monitor regulations/ethics/best practice.

**Guidance for users:** Supportive use only (AI assists, never replaces, human authors); disclaimers when AI-assisted content is used (scope + limitations); internal policies for data handling, model validation, QA; documented safe vs unsafe practices.

**Conclusion:** "AI will not replace human authors, but it is reshaping technical writing... The key challenge is no longer adoption, but responsible use: maintaining transparency, accountability, and strict adherence to STE principles and rules."

## 13. Side quest A — FAA 1996 Simplified English study

- The FAA OAM tech-reports index (https://www.faa.gov/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports) returned 403 to WebFetch; a curl fetch with a browser UA succeeded but the page is a navigation/search shell (reports database is dynamic) with **no static mention of Simplified English** — the study is not directly listed there.
- The study was located via Crossref instead: **Chervak, S., Drury, C. G., & Ouellette, J. P. (1996). "Simplified English for Aircraft Workcards." Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society 40th Annual Meeting** (Oct 1996). DOI: 10.1177/154193129604000502. (This is the FAA-sponsored human-factors research on Simplified English effectiveness commonly cited in STE literature.)
- **Design:** 16 workcards crossing two levels of difficulty (Easy/Difficult) x two levels of language (Simplified English / Non-SE) x two levels of document layout (standard/revised), between-subjects, comprehension assessments.
- **Participants:** 175 practicing Aircraft Maintenance Technicians.
- **Findings:** (1) "Comprehension was significantly improved with Simplified English, particularly for the Difficult workcards and for non-native English speakers." (2) The SE benefit was strongest for complex material. (3) "No effects of layout were found" — language, not presentation format, drove comprehension.
- Net: empirical support for adopting Simplified English in aviation maintenance documentation, with the biggest gains for non-native speakers and difficult content.

## Synthesis — what the site establishes about STE's writing-rule and dictionary philosophy

The site does not publish the 53 rules themselves (they are in the free-on-request PDF), but across About + FAQ it documents the principles:

1. **53 writing rules in 9 sections** covering word choice, grammar, sentence structure, style.
2. **~900 approved words**, each with **one meaning and one part of speech** ("one word, one meaning"); ~1,200 non-approved words with approved alternatives; spelling/meanings per American English / Merriam-Webster.
3. **One synonym survives**: start, not begin/commence/initiate/originate; selection criteria = simplicity + frequency of use.
4. **Technical nouns (technical names) and technical verbs** are the open-vocabulary escape hatch — defined by subject-field categories, sourced from official documentation/drawings/glossaries/term bases; a non-approved word inside a technical noun/verb becomes acceptable as part of it (only such path; use sparingly).
5. **Imperative, active voice in procedures** ("Install the component"); passive banned in procedures, allowed in description only when the agent is unknown.
6. **Conditions come first** in work steps ("If hot oil touches your skin, injuries can occur").
7. **"-ing" forms generally prohibited** (ambiguity across parts of speech), with listed exceptions (opening, remaining, something, during) and inside technical nouns.
8. **Short sentences, one topic per sentence** (named in FAQ as portable principles; exact length caps are inside the standard).
9. **Reader-first asymmetry**: easy to read, demanding to write — C1 English recommended for writers; readers need no training.
10. **The standard is the only authority**: free PDF on request; tools optional and unendorsed; AI must support, never override, the rules; training (STEMG/UNINETTUNO-accredited) is the critical first step because untrained writers over-focus on the dictionary and neglect the rules.
